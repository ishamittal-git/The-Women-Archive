Message from the Founder

Welcome to The Women Archive.

This magazine began with a simple belief: women's stories deserve space. Not just the stories that make headlines, but the quiet ones too — the experiences, questions, ambitions, memories and ideas that often remain unheard.

The Women Archive is a space for young women to write, create, question and be seen. Through essays, poetry, interviews, art and conversations, we hope to build an archive that reflects the many ways women understand and experience the world.

We are still at the beginning, and that is what makes this so exciting. This is an archive we are building together, one story and one voice at a time.

Thank you for being here, for reading, and for making room for these stories.

Isha Mittal
Founder & Editor-in-Chief · The Women Archive

About Us

T

he Women Archive is an independent, youth-led magazine created as a space for women’s stories, ideas and perspectives to be heard, remembered and shared.

We believe an archive does not have to be made only of history. It can be made in the present, too — through the essays we write, the questions we ask, the art we create, the conversations we begin and the experiences we choose not to keep silent.

From personal essays and poetry to interviews, cultural commentary and visual work, the magazine brings together different ways of seeing and telling. There is room here for the loud stories, the quiet ones and everything in between.

WHY WE EXIST
OUR BELIEF Every voice has a story worth making room for.

We want The Women Archive to feel less like a publication you simply read and more like a growing collection you can leave something with.

A place where a first-time writer can submit a thought beside an experienced storyteller, where a poem can sit beside an interview, and where ordinary experiences can be treated as worthy of attention.

About the Founder

I sha Mittal is a journalist, writer, and poet currently pursuing an MA in Journalism at the Delhi School of Journalism, University of Delhi, following a BA (Hons) in English from Miranda House. She works as a News Writer with the HULAHUL Foundation and as a Journalist with ONE AIR MEDIA, and has previously served as an editor at The World Times and mentored young minds at Agamya: The Mentor-Mentee Club for Social Outreach, Miranda House.

Her published works span poetry collections, research papers and book chapters exploring emotions, gender, literature and popular culture — a thread of curiosity that runs through everything she writes. Her words have found homes in Samaj Weekly, The Chai Magazine, The Times of India and Hindustan Times.

"I started this magazine with a simple thought: there are so many women with stories, observations and ideas that deserve a page."

Learning to Say No

Anonymous · 6 min read

There is a strange discomfort that comes with saying “no.” We worry that we might disappoint someone, appear selfish, or damage a relationship. So, instead of saying what we truly mean, we say “yes” when we want to say “no.”

We agree to plans when we are exhausted. We take on responsibilities when our schedules are already full. We listen when we need space. We help when we have nothing left to give. Somewhere along the way, saying yes becomes easier than being honest.

But learning to say no is not about becoming cold or inconsiderate. It is about understanding that our time, energy and boundaries matter too.

Why Is Saying No So Difficult?

For many of us, saying no comes with guilt. We have been taught to be helpful, accommodating and available. Especially when we care about people, refusing a request can feel like letting them down.

Sometimes, we are afraid of being disliked. Sometimes, we think we need a “good enough” reason to refuse. And sometimes, we simply do not know how to disappoint someone without feeling guilty about it.

The irony is that constantly saying yes does not necessarily make us better friends, colleagues or people. It can leave us overwhelmed, frustrated and emotionally exhausted.

A No Does Not Need an Explanation

One of the hardest lessons is understanding that you do not always need to justify your boundaries.

“I can't make it today” can be enough.

“I don't have the capacity for this right now” can be enough.

“I'd rather not” can be enough.

We often feel the need to create elaborate explanations because we want our no to be accepted. But a boundary does not become valid only when someone else agrees with it.

Saying No Without Being Cruel

Saying no does not have to mean being rude. You can be firm while still being kind.

Instead of disappearing from a conversation or responding harshly, you can simply say:

“Thank you for asking, but I won't be able to take this on.”

“I appreciate the invitation, but I need some time for myself.”

“I understand that this is important to you, but I can't commit to it right now.”

These sentences may feel uncomfortable at first. With practice, however, they become easier.

The Guilt Might Stay

Perhaps the most important thing to remember is that feeling guilty does not mean you have made the wrong decision.

You can feel guilty and still have a valid boundary.

You can care about someone and still refuse their request.

You can love your friends and still need time away from them.

You can want to help and still recognise that you cannot do everything.

The goal is not necessarily to stop feeling guilty overnight. The goal is to stop allowing guilt to make every decision for you.

Start Small

You do not have to suddenly become someone who refuses everything.

Start with small things.

Decline an invitation when you genuinely want to stay home. Say no to an unnecessary commitment. Take a moment before automatically agreeing to a request.

Instead of immediately saying, “Sure, I'll do it,” try saying, “Let me check and get back to you.”

That small pause can make a difference. It gives you time to ask yourself: Do I actually want to do this? Do I have the time? Am I agreeing because I want to, or because I am afraid of saying no?

Your Time Is Yours Too

There will always be someone who needs something from you. Another message to answer. Another favour to do. Another responsibility to accept.

If you keep giving without considering yourself, eventually there may be nothing left to give.

Learning to say no is therefore not simply about refusing other people. It is about saying yes to yourself.

Yes to rest.

Yes to your priorities.

Yes to your peace.

Yes to the things that genuinely matter to you.

And perhaps that is the real lesson behind learning to say no: you are not responsible for being available to everyone, all the time.

Sometimes, the kindest thing you can do for yourself is simply say, “No, I can't.”

And let that be enough.
